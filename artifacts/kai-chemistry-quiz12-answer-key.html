What Went Wrong on Quiz 12

Root Cause Analysis
5.1.1 + 5.1.2 Electron Domain Counting: The Double Bond Error

On the quiz, Kai drew CS2 and identified 3 bonding domains and trigonal planar shape. This is wrong. CS2 is linear, just like CO2. The root cause: counting each bond line as a separate domain instead of counting each bonded atom.

The Only Rule That Matters
Count how many other atoms are bonded to the central atom. That is the number of bonding domains - regardless of whether those bonds are single, double, or triple. One double bond = 1 domain. One triple bond = 1 domain.
CS2 (what went wrong on quiz): S=C=S. C is bonded to 2 atoms (both S). That is 2 bonding domains. No lone pairs on C. Total = 2. Shape = LINEAR. Angle = 180°.

Same logic, CO2: O=C=O. C bonded to 2 atoms. 2 domains, 0 LP. LINEAR. 180°.

The double bond is irrelevant to the domain count. Only the number of connected atoms matters.

Quick check Kai can use: draw the Lewis structure, circle the central atom, and ask "how many other atoms have lines touching this circle?" That is the bonding domain count.

5.1.3 3-D Drawing: Wedge-Dash Notation

Wedge-dash notation communicates which bonds point toward the viewer, away, or across the paper. Every 3-D structure has exactly the same logical rules.

Three Bond Types
Solid wedge (filled triangle) - bond coming toward you, out of the page.
Dashed lines - bond going away from you, behind the page.
Regular line - bond in the plane of the paper, going left or right or up/down.
CH4 (tetrahedral): Draw C at center. Put H-top and H-left as regular lines (in the plane). Draw H-front-right as a solid wedge angling toward viewer. Draw H-back-right as a dashed line angling away. The four bonds should look like they extend in 4 different directions in 3-D space.

Tip: For tetrahedral molecules, a common setup is two line bonds (left/right or up/down) plus one wedge (lower-front) and one dash (lower-back). Practice drawing this until it becomes automatic before the re-test.

5.1.6 Polarity Arrows: Direction and Cancellation

The quiz score suggests arrows were present but the direction, labeling, or cancellation analysis was incomplete.

Arrow Convention
The polarity arrow points from the atom with lower EN (δ+) toward the atom with higher EN (δ−). It always points toward the more electronegative atom. Label δ+ at the tail and δ− at the arrowhead.

For the molecule overall: if arrows are equal and opposite (symmetric) - they cancel and the molecule is nonpolar. If they point in the same or similar direction - there is a net dipole and the molecule is polar.
CO2: Both C=O bonds have arrows pointing away from C toward each O. But the two arrows point in exactly opposite directions (left and right). They cancel. Net dipole = 0. Nonpolar molecule.

H2O: Both O-H bonds have arrows pointing toward O (more EN). The molecule is bent, so both arrows point "up and inward" toward O. They reinforce each other. Net dipole points toward O. Polar molecule.
5.1.7 Lone Pair Effect: Why Angles Get Compressed

Lone pairs are not just "missing bonds" - they occupy more space than bonding pairs and push neighboring bonds closer together.

Repulsion Hierarchy (most to least)
LP−LP repulsion > LP−BP repulsion > BP−BP repulsion

Each lone pair you add to the central atom compresses the bond angles further. This is why:
CH4 (0 LP): 109.5° → NH3 (1 LP): ~107° → H2O (2 LP): ~104.5°

Mnemonic: "Lone pairs are territorial. They take up more space and squeeze the bonded pairs closer together." Every LP you add to the central atom costs about 2-3 degrees off the bond angle.

5.2.2 IMF Identification: The Hydrogen Bonding Rule

The most common error in IMF identification is applying hydrogen bonding to molecules that do not qualify. The rule is very specific.

Hydrogen Bonding Requires H Bonded Directly to N, O, or F
Not just "near" N, O, or F. The H atom must be in a N-H, O-H, or F-H bond specifically.
CH3F: Contains F. But where is H? Bonded to C. Not to F. So CH3F has only LDF + DD. No hydrogen bonding.

HF: H is bonded directly to F. This qualifies. HF has LDF + DD + HB.

CH3OH (methanol): One H is bonded directly to O (the O-H group). This qualifies. Has LDF + DD + HB.

Rule check: Look for the pattern N-H, O-H, or F-H in the structural formula. If the H is bonded to any other atom (like C), no HB.
5.2.3 Properties from IMFs: Size vs. Strength

The quiz asked which molecule had the highest boiling point. The error was likely comparing molecules with different IMF types without accounting for molecular size. LDF strength scales with molecular size - a very large nonpolar molecule can have stronger LDF than a smaller polar molecule with HB.

Boiling Point Decision Tree
1. Identify all IMFs for each molecule.
2. Compare IMF types: HB > DD > LDF.
3. If both molecules have the same IMF type, the larger molecule has stronger LDF and higher bp.
4. If types differ, check if the size advantage of the nonpolar molecule is large enough to overcome the type disadvantage.
Key insight: For molecules of similar size, HB wins. But hexane (C6H14, 86 g/mol, LDF only, bp = 69°C) beats methanol (CH3OH, 32 g/mol, HB, bp = 65°C) because hexane is so much larger - its LDF is very strong despite having no HB.

Lone Pair Effects on Bond Angles
Q1: Why is the H-O-H angle in H2O approximately 104.5° instead of the ideal 109.5°?
H2O has 2 lone pairs on the central oxygen atom. Lone pairs repel bonding pairs more strongly than bonding pairs repel each other (LP−BP > BP−BP repulsion). With two lone pairs, they push the two O-H bonds inward, compressing the angle from the ideal 109.5° down to approximately 104.5°. Each lone pair takes up more angular space than a bonding pair, leaving less room for the H atoms.
Each lone pair added to the central atom costs roughly 2-3° off the bond angle.
Q2: Rank CH4, NH3, H2O from largest to smallest bond angle.
Ranking: CH4 (109.5°) > NH3 (~107°) > H2O (~104.5°)

All three have 4 total electron domains (tetrahedral geometry). The difference is the number of lone pairs on the central atom: CH4 has 0 LP so ideal angle; NH3 has 1 LP compressing it slightly; H2O has 2 LP compressing it the most.
Q3: Why is BCl3 exactly 120° but SO2 is less than 120°?
Both molecules have 3 electron domains on the central atom (trigonal planar geometry). BCl3 has 3 bonding domains and 0 lone pairs - all three positions are bonding pairs, so they spread out equally at exactly 120°. SO2 has 2 bonding domains and 1 lone pair on S. That lone pair repels the two S-O bonds more strongly than a bonded atom would, pushing the bond angle below 120° to approximately 119°.
Q4: BF3 vs NF3 - what is wrong with saying they have the same shape?
The error is ignoring the lone pairs on the central atom. BF3: B is in Group 13, forms 3 bonds, has NO lone pairs. 3 bonding domains, 0 LP = trigonal planar, 120°. NF3: N is in Group 15, forms 3 bonds, but has 1 lone pair. 3 bonding domains, 1 LP = trigonal pyramidal, ~107°. Even though both have 3 F atoms bonded to the central atom, the lone pair on N completely changes the 3-D geometry. The number of bonded atoms alone does not determine shape - you must count lone pairs too.
Shape = atom positions only. But angle = determined by ALL domains including lone pairs.
D
Polarity Arrows - Correct Answers

1. HCl

Arrow points H → Cl (Cl EN=3.0 > H EN=2.1). H is δ+, Cl is δ−. Net dipole: YES, toward Cl. This is a simple diatomic polar molecule - the single arrow IS the net dipole.

2. CO2 - Two arrows that CANCEL

Both C=O bonds are polar (O more EN than C). Arrow 1 points right; Arrow 2 points left. They are equal in magnitude and opposite in direction. They cancel perfectly. Net dipole: NO. Nonpolar molecule. This is why CO2 is nonpolar despite having polar bonds - linear symmetry cancels them.

3. NH3 - Three arrows all point toward N

N is more EN (3.0) than H (2.1). All three arrows point from H → N. Because the molecule is pyramidal (not flat), the arrows do NOT cancel - they all point generally "up toward N." Net dipole: YES, pointing toward N (upward).

4. CH2F2 - Asymmetric cancellation

C-H bonds are slightly polar (C more EN); arrows point from H toward C (small). C-F bonds are strongly polar (F EN=4.0 vs C EN=2.5); arrows point from C toward F (large). The two F arrows dominate and point downward. The molecule is asymmetric. Net dipole: YES, pointing toward the F atoms (downward).

Properties from IMFs - Answer Key
Q1: Which molecule (A-G) has the highest boiling point?
F (hexane, C6H14, bp = +69°C) has the highest boiling point. Although hexane only has LDF (no DD or HB), its large molecular size (86 g/mol, 6 carbons) creates strong London Dispersion Forces through greater surface area and polarizability. This is slightly higher than D (methanol, bp = +65°C) which has HB but is a much smaller molecule. The key lesson: large nonpolar molecules can have stronger LDF than small molecules with HB.
If you said D (methanol, +65°C), that is also reasonable and nearly correct - these two are very close. Full credit for either, but the reasoning must mention molecular size for F or HB for D.
Q2: Which molecule has the lowest boiling point?
A (Ar, bp = −186°C). Argon is a monatomic noble gas with only extremely weak LDF (tiny atom, no bonds to polarize). All other molecules in the table are larger or have stronger IMFs. Ar has the weakest IMFs and therefore the lowest boiling point by a large margin.
Q3: Would Ar be a solid, liquid, or gas at 25°C?
Gas. Ar has a boiling point of −186°C. At 25°C, we are 211°C above its boiling point - Ar is well into the gas phase. The reason Ar's boiling point is so low: it is a monatomic noble gas with only LDF, and those LDF are extremely weak because the atom has no bonds and minimal electron cloud. There is almost nothing holding Ar atoms together in a liquid or solid state at normal temperatures.
Q4: Both butane and hexane are nonpolar with only LDF. Why does hexane have a higher boiling point?
Hexane (C6H14, 86 g/mol) is a larger molecule than butane (C4H10, 58 g/mol). Both have only LDF, but LDF strength increases with molecular size. A larger molecule has more electrons, a greater surface area, and is more easily polarized (temporary dipoles are stronger). More electrons = stronger temporary dipole = stronger LDF = more energy needed to boil = higher boiling point.
Rule: When comparing nonpolar molecules, bigger molecule = stronger LDF = higher boiling point.
Q5: HF (20 g/mol, bp = +19.5°C) vs CH3F (34 g/mol, bp = −78°C) - why is HF's bp so much higher?
Despite being the smaller molecule, HF has a much higher boiling point because it has hydrogen bonding. HF contains an H atom bonded directly to F - a classic H-F bond - which qualifies for the strongest type of IMF (HB). CH3F has H bonded to C (not F), so it only has LDF + DD, no hydrogen bonding. HB in HF is strong enough to dramatically increase its boiling point (+19.5°C) far beyond what you would predict from its tiny size alone. CH3F, despite being heavier, lacks HB and has a much lower bp (−78°C).
This question is the HB rule in action. HF qualifies for HB. CH3F does not. That single difference explains the 97°C difference in boiling point.
