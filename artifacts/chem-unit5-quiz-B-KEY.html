Chemistry — Unit 5: Molecular Shape

Quiz [B] — Bonding Sites, Lone Pairs, Shape Names, and 3-D Drawing
Section 1 — Identify Bonding Sites and Lone Pairs (5.1.1)
1. CO2   (4 pts — 2 pts each)
a. Bonding sites on C: 2
b. Lone pairs on C: 0
Why:
CO2 is O=C=O. Even though there are two double bonds, each double bond still counts as ONE bonding site. C has 2 bonding sites and uses all 4 of its valence electrons in the two double bonds—zero left for lone pairs. Result: linear shape, 180° bond angle.
Common mistake to watch for: Students may count 4 bonding sites by counting each line in O=C=O. Remind Kai: it’s the number of bonded atoms attached, not the number of bond lines.
2. PCl3   (4 pts — 2 pts each)
a. Bonding sites on P: 3
b. Lone pairs on P: 1
Why:
Phosphorus has 5 valence electrons. It forms 3 bonds with the 3 Cl atoms (using 3 electrons). The remaining 2 electrons form 1 lone pair. 3 bonds + 1 lone pair = 4 electron pairs total = domain geometry is tetrahedral, molecular shape is trigonal pyramidal. PCl3 is structurally identical to NH3—same shape, different atoms.
3. NH3 vs. NF3   (6 pts — 1 pt per blank + 2 pts explanation)
a. NH3: bonding sites = 3   lone pairs = 1   shape = Trigonal Pyramidal
b. NF3: bonding sites = 3   lone pairs = 1   shape = Trigonal Pyramidal
c. Yes, they have the same shape. Molecular shape depends only on the number of bonding sites and lone pairs on the central atom. Both NH3 and NF3 have N with 3 bonding sites and 1 lone pair, so both are trigonal pyramidal. It doesn’t matter whether the bonded atoms are H or F.
Key insight for Marcus:
This is a powerful concept. The shape of a molecule is determined entirely by what’s on the central atom (bonds + lone pairs). The identity of the outer atoms changes bond polarity (covered in 5.1.4) but not the geometric shape.

Distinction Marcus should know: Trigonal Planar Bent vs. Tetrahedral Bent
Both are called “bent,” but they come from different domain geometries. Trigonal planar bent (2 bonds, 1 lone pair) has a domain geometry of trigonal planar—all 3 electron pairs in one plane, bond angle <120°. Example: SO2. Tetrahedral bent (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs) has a domain geometry of tetrahedral—bond angle ~104.5°. Example: H2O. Kai may confuse these two—the key is counting lone pairs.
5. H2S vs. H2O   (6 pts)
Full credit answer:
Yes, the student is correct about the shape. Both H2S and H2O have a central atom with 2 bonding sites and 2 lone pairs, so both are tetrahedral bent (bent). The VSEPR table predicts a bond angle of ~104.5° for this category (based on H2O). Accept 104.5° as the answer at this level. Note for Marcus: the actual measured angle of H2S is closer to 92° because sulfur is a larger period-3 atom and uses unhybridized p-orbitals, which are naturally 90°. H2O follows the VSEPR table closely; H2S deviates significantly. Kai does not need to know the 92° value for this standard.
Scoring guide:
2 pts — States student is correct; 2 pts — Names shape (bent / tetrahedral bent); 2 pts — Explains lone pair compression effect on bond angle

6. Mystery molecule (2 bonds, 2 lone pairs)   (6 pts — 1.5 pts each)
a. Domain geometry: Tetrahedral
b. Molecular shape: Tetrahedral Bent (Bent)
c. Bond angle: 104.5°
d. Example molecule: H2O  (also accept H2S)
Domain geometry vs. molecular shape explained:
Domain geometry = the arrangement of ALL electron pairs (bonds + lone pairs). Molecular shape = the arrangement of the ATOMS only. For H2O: 4 electron pairs → domain is tetrahedral. But only 2 bonds exist between atoms, so the shape seen is bent. This two-name system is tested constantly in Unit 5.
Section 3 — Draw 3-D Structures (5.1.3)
7. CF4 — Tetrahedral 3-D Drawing   (8 pts)

Same structure as CH4—F replaces H at each bond end. Tetrahedral: 4 bonds, 0 lone pairs, 109.5°.

Scoring rubric (8 pts):
1 pt — Wedge bond (solid filled triangle) present and pointing toward viewer
1 pt — Dashed bond (dashed line) present and pointing away from viewer
2 pts — Two regular bonds in the plane of the paper
4 pts — F atom at each of the 4 bond ends (1 pt each)
Accept any valid tetrahedral orientation. The shape of CF4 is identical to CH4—this problem tests whether Kai understands that atom identity doesn’t change geometry.
8. H2O — Tetrahedral Bent 3-D Drawing   (10 pts)
Sample explanation (full credit):
“H2O has a bond angle of 104.5° rather than 109.5° because it has 2 lone pairs on oxygen, while CH4 has 0 lone pairs. Lone pairs repel more strongly than bonding pairs, pushing the 2 H atoms closer together and compressing the H–O–H angle below the tetrahedral ideal of 109.5°.”
Scoring rubric (10 pts):
3 pts — Drawing shows bent shape (O in center, 2 H bonds angled downward)
3 pts — Bond types labeled (wedge + dash, or wedge + regular, or dash + regular)
2 pts — Both lone pairs shown/labeled above O
2 pts — Explanation references lone pairs causing angle compression below 109.5°
