How to use this: Try each problem on the practice test FIRST. Then scroll here to check your work. Each question has a hint (yellow box) and the full answer (green box) below it.
Question 4: Is the Formula Neutral?
Q4: Mg(OH)3 and K2SO4
Two compounds with polyatomic ions. Only one has the proper formula. Identify the polyatomic ion in each and explain why each is or is not correct.
Hint

Look at the polyatomic ion reference table. What is the charge of hydroxide (OH)? What is the charge of sulfate (SO4)?

Now look at the metal. What group is Mg in? What group is K in? What charge does each prefer?

For each compound: multiply the metal's charge by how many there are. Multiply the polyatomic ion's charge by how many there are. Add them up. Does it equal zero?

Full Answer

Mg(OH)3

Find the charges. Mg is in group 2A, so its preferred charge is +2. OH (hydroxide) is on the reference table as 1-.
Count them. There is 1 Mg and 3 OH (the subscript 3 outside the parentheses).
Multiply. Mg: (+2)(1) = +2. OH: (-1)(3) = -3.
Add. +2 + (-3) = -1. Not zero, so NOT neutral.
The correct formula would be Mg(OH)2 because (+2) + (-1)(2) = 0.

K2SO4

Find the charges. K is in group 1A: +1. SO4 (sulfate) is on the reference table as 2-.
Count them. 2 K and 1 SO4.
Multiply. K: (+1)(2) = +2. SO4: (-2)(1) = -2.
Add. +2 + (-2) = 0. This IS neutral. Correct formula.
Mg(OH)3 = NOT correct. K2SO4 = correct.
Remember

An ionic compound must be electrically neutral. The total positive charge has to exactly cancel the total negative charge. If the charges don't add to zero, the formula is wrong.

Question 5: Compound Analysis
Q5: Ca(NO3)2
Circle cation/anion, fill in charges, count atoms.
Hint

The cation is the positive ion (the metal). The anion is the negative ion. Which one is the metal here?

For the charge of nitrate: don't guess. Look it up on the reference table. It's listed under "(1-) Anion".

For atom counting: the subscript 2 outside the parentheses multiplies EVERYTHING inside. So (NO3)2 means 2 copies of NO3.

Full Answer

Cation or Anion:

Calcium (Ca) is a metal (group 2A). Metals form positive ions. Ca is the cation.
Nitrate (NO3) is a polyatomic ion on the reference table. It's negative. NO3 is the anion.

Charges:

Reference table: Nitrate (NO3) is under "(1-) Anion". Its charge is 1-.
Ca is group 2A. Its preferred charge is 2+.
Check: (+2) + (-1)(2) = 0. Neutral.

Atom count:

Ca: 1 (just one calcium)
N: 2 (1 nitrogen inside parentheses x 2 outside = 2)
O: 6 (3 oxygens inside parentheses x 2 outside = 6)
Cation: Ca. Anion: NO3. Nitrate charge: 1-. Calcium charge: 2+. Atoms: Ca=1, N=2, O=6.
Common Mistake

Nitrate (NO3) is 1-, not 2-. A lot of students mix this up because carbonate (CO3) and sulfate (SO4) are both 2-. But nitrate is only 1-. When in doubt, check the reference table.

Question 6: Write the Neutral Formula
Q6, Row 1: Sodium (Na) and Chlorine (Cl)
Hint

Na is in group 1A. Cl is in group 7A. What charge does each prefer? If both charges are the same size, what ratio do you need?

Full Answer
Na is group 1A: charge = +1. Cl is group 7A: charge = -1.
Equal and opposite, so 1:1 ratio.
Na+ | Cl- | NaCl
Q6, Row 2: Barium (Ba) and Oxygen (O)
Hint

Ba is in group 2A. O is in group 6A. Both charges are size 2. What ratio?

Full Answer
Ba is group 2A: charge = +2. O is group 6A: charge = -2.
Equal and opposite, so 1:1 ratio.
Ba2+ | O2- | BaO
Q6, Row 3: Li+ and OH-
Hint

The charges are already given: Li is +1, OH is -1. What ratio balances to zero? Do you need parentheses around OH?

You only need parentheses around a polyatomic ion when there is more than one of it.

Full Answer
Li+ is +1. OH- is -1. Ratio: 1:1.
Only one hydroxide, so no parentheses needed.
Li+ | OH- | LiOH
Parentheses Rule

Only put parentheses around a polyatomic ion when you need MORE THAN ONE of it. LiOH has just one OH, so no parentheses. Ca(OH)2 has two OH groups, so you need parentheses.

Q6, Row 4: Cu2+ and CO32-
Hint

Both charges are already given: Cu is 2+ and CO3 is 2-. Think about the ratio. Do you need more than one of either ion?

Full Answer
Cu2+ is +2. CO32- is -2. Equal and opposite.
1:1 ratio. Only one carbonate, so no parentheses.
Cu2+ | CO32- | CuCO3
Question 7: Name the Compound / Write the Formula
Naming Rules to Remember

Type I (simple metal + nonmetal): Write the metal name, then the nonmetal root + "-ide". Example: NaCl = sodium chloride.

Type II (transition metal + nonmetal): Same as Type I, but add a Roman numeral after the metal name to show its charge. Example: FeCl2 = iron(II) chloride. You figure out the charge from the formula.

Type III (contains a polyatomic ion): Use the metal name + the polyatomic ion's name from the reference table. Don't add "-ide" because the polyatomic ion already has its own name. Example: Ca(NO3)2 = calcium nitrate.

Q7, Row 1: K2O → Name?
Hint

Is potassium a transition metal? (No, group 1A.) So is this Type I or Type II? What's the nonmetal? Change its ending to "-ide".

Important: is this an ionic compound (metal + nonmetal) or a covalent compound (nonmetal + nonmetal)? The naming rules are completely different for each.

Full Answer
K is potassium (group 1A, not a transition metal). O is oxygen.
Type I: simple metal + nonmetal. No Roman numeral needed.
Metal name: potassium. Nonmetal root + -ide: oxide.
potassium oxide
Why NOT "dipotassium monoxide"?

Prefixes like mono-, di-, tri- are only for covalent (molecular) compounds, which are made of two nonmetals. Examples: CO2 = carbon dioxide, N2O5 = dinitrogen pentoxide.

K2O is an ionic compound (metal + nonmetal), so you NEVER use those prefixes. You just say the metal name + nonmetal-ide. The subscripts come automatically from the charges (K is always +1, O is always -2, so you always need 2 K to balance 1 O). Since the ratio is always the same, you don't need to say "two" or "one."

The Big Rule: When Do You Use Prefixes?

Ionic compounds (metal + nonmetal): NEVER use mono-, di-, tri-. The charges already tell you the ratio. Just say the names.

Covalent compounds (nonmetal + nonmetal): ALWAYS use prefixes (mono-, di-, tri-, tetra-, penta-) because nonmetals can combine in different ratios. CO is carbon monoxide. CO2 is carbon dioxide. They are different compounds, so you need the prefix to tell them apart.

Quick check: Is the first element a metal? If yes, it's ionic. No prefixes. Is the first element a nonmetal? Then it's covalent. Use prefixes.

Q7, Row 2: MgBr2 → Name?
Hint

Mg is in group 2A. Is it a transition metal? What's the naming pattern for non-transition metals?

Think about whether this is ionic or covalent. Does that change whether you use prefixes?

Full Answer
Mg is magnesium (group 2A, not a transition metal). Br is bromine.
Type I. No Roman numeral.
Metal: magnesium. Nonmetal root + -ide: bromide.
magnesium bromide
Why NOT "magnesium dibromide"?

Same rule as K2O above. Mg is a metal and Br is a nonmetal, so MgBr2 is ionic. No prefixes for ionic compounds.

Mg is ALWAYS +2. Br is ALWAYS -1. So you ALWAYS need 2 Br to balance 1 Mg. Since there's only one possible ratio, saying "di" adds no information. Everyone already knows it's 2 bromides.

Compare to covalent: NO2 is nitrogen dioxide but N2O4 is dinitrogen tetroxide. Both are nitrogen + oxygen, but the ratios are different. That's why covalent compounds need prefixes and ionic compounds don't.

Q7, Row 3: FeCl2 → Name?
Hint

Fe (iron) is a transition metal. It can have different charges. You need to figure out which charge iron has in THIS compound.

Use the anion to work backwards: what's the charge of Cl? If there are 2 Cl atoms, what's the total negative charge? What must Fe's charge be to make it neutral?

Full Answer
Fe is iron (transition metal). Cl is chlorine (group 7A, charge = -1).
2 Cl atoms. Total negative charge: (-1)(2) = -2.
Formula must be neutral, so Fe's charge must be +2.
Type II: transition metal, MUST include Roman numeral.
Iron is +2, so Roman numeral (II). Cl root + -ide = chloride.
iron(II) chloride
Common Mistake

If you just write "iron chloride" without the (II), it's wrong. Iron can be +2 or +3, so you MUST say which one. The Roman numeral is not optional for transition metals.

Q7, Row 4: Ca(OH)2 → Name?
Hint

Does this compound contain a polyatomic ion? Check the reference table. If it does, use the polyatomic ion's name directly. Don't change it to "-ide".

Full Answer
Ca is calcium (group 2A, not a transition metal). OH is hydroxide (on the reference table).
Type III: contains a polyatomic ion.
Metal: calcium. Polyatomic ion name: hydroxide (use it as-is).
Ca is NOT a transition metal, so no Roman numeral.
calcium hydroxide
Q7, Row 5: (NH4)3PO4 → Name?
Hint

This one has TWO polyatomic ions. Look at the reference table. What is NH4 called? What is PO4 called? Just use both names.

Full Answer
NH4 is ammonium (the only polyatomic cation). PO4 is phosphate.
Type III: contains polyatomic ions.
Cation: ammonium. Anion: phosphate.
ammonium phosphate
Q7, Row 6: Potassium nitrate → Formula?
Hint

Potassium is K. "Nitrate" is a polyatomic ion. Look up its formula and charge on the reference table. Then figure out the ratio that makes it neutral.

Full Answer
Potassium = K, group 1A, charge = +1.
Nitrate = NO3, from the reference table, charge = 1-.
+1 and -1: 1:1 ratio. Only one nitrate, no parentheses.
KNO3
Q7, Row 7: Copper(II) sulfate → Formula?
Hint

The "(II)" tells you copper's charge is +2. "Sulfate" is a polyatomic ion. Look up sulfate's charge. Are they the same size?

Full Answer
Copper(II) = Cu, charge = +2 (the II tells you).
Sulfate = SO4, from the reference table, charge = 2-.
Both 2: 1:1 ratio. Only one sulfate, no parentheses.
CuSO4
Reading Roman Numerals in Names

When a compound name includes (II) or (III), that tells you the metal's charge. You don't need to figure it out. I = +1, II = +2, III = +3.

Q7, Row 8: Aluminum chloride → Formula?
Hint

Al is in group 3A. "Chloride" means Cl with a -1 charge. How many Cl atoms do you need to balance Al's charge?

Full Answer
Aluminum = Al, group 3A, charge = +3.
"Chloride" = Cl- (the "-ide" tells you it's a single element, charge = -1).
To balance +3, need 3 chloride: (-1)(3) = -3. Total: +3 + (-3) = 0.
AlCl3
Q7, Row 9: Barium phosphate → Formula?
Hint

Ba is group 2A (charge = +2). "Phosphate" is a polyatomic ion. Look up its charge. The charges are different sizes this time, so you'll need the "criss-cross" method: the number from one charge becomes the subscript of the other.

Full Answer
Barium = Ba, group 2A, charge = +2.
Phosphate = PO4, from the reference table, charge = 3-.
Different charges (+2 and -3). Criss-cross:
 The 3 from phosphate becomes Ba's subscript: Ba3
 The 2 from barium becomes PO4's subscript: (PO4)2
Check: (+2)(3) + (-3)(2) = +6 + (-6) = 0. Neutral.
Parentheses needed around PO4 because there are 2 of them.
Ba3(PO4)2
Common Mistake

When you criss-cross, don't forget parentheses around the polyatomic ion if the subscript is greater than 1. Writing BaPO42 would mean something completely different.

Quick Reference: The Three Types
Type When How to name Example
I Metal has ONE charge (groups 1A, 2A, 3A) metal + nonmetal root + "-ide" NaCl = sodium chloride
II Transition metal (multiple charges) metal + (Roman numeral) + root + "-ide" FeCl2 = iron(II) chloride
III Has a polyatomic ion metal + polyatomic ion name Ca(NO3)2 = calcium nitrate
