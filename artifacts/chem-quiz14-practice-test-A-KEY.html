Part B: Coefficients vs Subscripts (6.1.2, 6.1.3)
You can CHANGE the coefficient (the number in front of a formula) because it changes how many molecules of that substance react or are produced, without changing what the substance is. You CANNOT change the subscript because that would change the identity of the compound itself (e.g., H2O vs H2O2 are completely different substances). Changing subscripts violates conservation of mass because you'd be creating a different molecule.

Part B: Define a Mole (6.2.2)
A mole is a counting unit equal to 6.022 × 1023 particles (Avogadro's number). It represents a specific COUNT of atoms, molecules, or formula units. It is needed in chemistry because atoms are too small to count individually; the mole bridges the gap between the microscopic world (individual atoms/molecules) and the macroscopic world (grams that we can measure on a balance).

6.3.4: Limiting Reagent Problem
N2(g) + 3 H2(g) → 2 NH3(g)
Step 1: Convert both to moles
N2: 5.0 g / 28.02 g/mol = 0.1785 mol N2
H2: 5.0 g / 2.016 g/mol = 2.480 mol H2

Step 2: Find limiting reagent
N2 needs: 0.1785 × 3 = 0.5355 mol H2 (we have 2.480 — plenty)
H2 needs: 2.480 / 3 = 0.827 mol N2 (we only have 0.1785 — not enough!)
N2 is the Limiting Reagent
H2 is the Excess Reagent

Step 3: Maximum yield of NH3
0.1785 mol N2 × (2 mol NH3 / 1 mol N2) = 0.357 mol NH3
0.357 mol × 17.03 g/mol = 6.08 g NH3

Step 4: Excess H2 remaining
H2 used: 0.1785 × 3 = 0.5355 mol × 2.016 g/mol = 1.08 g
Remaining: 5.0 − 1.08 = 3.92 g H2 remaining
